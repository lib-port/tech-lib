# Chapter 3: The Strategic Context For Business Analysis

## Strategy and business analysis

Strategy establishes an organisation's long-term direction and connects its goals with actions and resources. The planning horizon depends on its circumstances. A technology start-up may look one or two years ahead, while a power generator may plan across several decades. Tactics are the specific actions through which strategy is executed.

Effective strategy connects organisational ambitions with internal capabilities and external conditions. It supports sustained performance, while poor strategic choices can gradually weaken an organisation. Execution develops the capabilities required to achieve the intended outcomes.

Business analysts contribute by connecting proposed changes with strategic priorities. This understanding helps them assess decisions, challenge assumptions, evaluate expected benefits, and work credibly with stakeholders. Without it, a project may satisfy an immediate requirement while damaging the organisation's longer-term capabilities.

Strategic analysis connects three elements: the current state, the desired target state, and the roadmap between them. Performance measurement supplies feedback on progress and informs adjustments to strategy and execution.

## Developing strategy

The approach to strategy development reflects organisational culture, size, complexity, risk tolerance, and stakeholder expectations.

Adaptive development uses repeated feedback from markets and stakeholders. Organisations introduce viable offerings, learn from experience, and adjust their products or services. This approach supports experimentation and continuous improvement, but frequent changes can complicate communication. It may be unsuitable where failure has serious consequences or market feedback offers limited guidance.

It is often associated with employee empowerment, openness to feedback, and evidence-based decisions. Continual learning depends on the organisation's willingness to revise earlier assumptions.

Linear development establishes a relatively stable sequence of goals, plans, and implementation. It can suit stable conditions and offers a consistent direction that is easier to communicate. Its weakness is potentially slow adjustment when circumstances or customer preferences change.

Hybrid development combines these approaches. A product development team may experiment while another part of the organisation follows a stable plan for established services.

## Understanding the external environment

External analysis identifies opportunities and threats. PESTLE examines wider conditions, while Porter's Five Forces examines industry relationships.

### PESTLE

PESTLE groups influences beyond an organisation's immediate control into six categories.

| Factor | Relevant influences |
| --- | --- |
| Political | Government policies, international institutions, and political campaigns. |
| Economic | Interest rates, inflation, employment, disposable income, resource costs, growth, and exchange rates. |
| Socio-cultural | Demographics, habits, preferences, and public attitudes. |
| Technological | New technologies, automation, connected systems, digital services, and technical skills. |
| Legal | Employment, privacy, competition, taxation, safety, equality, intellectual property, and sector-specific requirements. |
| Environmental | Climate change, emissions, habitats, animal welfare, waste, and recycling. |

Recognising a trend supports informed decisions without guaranteeing success. An appliance manufacturer, for example, may need to assess growing demand for energy-efficient, connected products.

### Porter's Five Forces

The five forces reveal different sources of competitive pressure.

- Competitive rivalry depends on competitors' numbers, relative strength, differentiation, market growth, and exit barriers.
- Supplier power reflects supplier size, brand strength, switching costs, and purchasing arrangements.
- Buyer power reflects customer size, available alternatives, switching costs, and the ability to provide the offering independently.
- New entrants face barriers such as capital requirements, regulation, distribution access, brand recognition, and operational capabilities.
- Substitutes meet the underlying need differently, such as public transport replacing car ownership or streaming replacing theatre visits.

Scale changes entry barriers. Establishing a global restaurant chain requires premises, staff, supply relationships, and consistent processes across locations. Opening one local restaurant generally requires fewer resources. Strong brands and costly supplier changes can further affect negotiating power.

The framework can be harder to apply to not-for-profit organisations, complex international markets, or organisations that occupy several roles. Digital platforms can also challenge assumed entry barriers by using assets or infrastructure owned by others. Blue Ocean Strategy seeks new demand and less contested markets by reshaping market boundaries through different business models.

## Assessing the organisation

Internal analysis identifies strengths and weaknesses in direction, resources, performance, and the product or service portfolio.

### Direction and resources

VMOST connects five elements of strategic direction. Vision defines the desired future state. Mission describes what the organisation does or intends to do. Objectives specify the outcomes sought. Strategy establishes the long-term approach, and tactics define the detailed actions.

These elements need to be clear, understood, and mutually consistent. Objectives are most useful when they are specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound, commonly abbreviated as SMART.

A resource audit examines physical assets, finances, people, know-how, and reputation. It assesses whether resources support the strategy, where constraints exist, and how well essential knowledge is retained. Skilled employees, sound cash flow, and strong customer confidence may be strengths. Unsuitable equipment, weak succession arrangements, or dependence on undocumented knowledge may be weaknesses.

Financial analysis considers assets, liabilities, borrowing capacity, and income and cost trends. Knowledge analysis examines whether expertise is accessible and retained beyond particular individuals. Reputation is assessed through stakeholder feedback, reviews, accreditations, and comparable performance.

### Performance and balance

The balanced scorecard connects vision and strategy with four perspectives: financial performance, internal business processes, learning and growth, and customers. It helps reveal weaknesses that financial results alone may conceal, such as declining service quality or insufficient investment in skills.

Process measures may include errors and delivery times. Customer measures may include complaints, loyalty, and recommendations. Learning and growth measures assess employee development, new offerings, and improvement initiatives.

Performance measurement links desired outcomes with evidence of progress.

- Objectives define the outcomes the organisation seeks.
- Critical success factors (CSFs) describe the conditions essential to achieving those outcomes.
- Key performance indicators (KPIs) quantify performance against those factors.
- Targets specify the required level of achievement.

Targets can distinguish performance significantly below expectations, slightly below them, and at or above them, often represented by red, amber, and green. A balanced measurement system links operational evidence with strategic objectives.

One objective can depend on several CSFs, and one KPI can inform more than one factor. For a tuition business, teaching quality and student engagement may both influence examination results. Session ratings could provide evidence about both factors, alongside measures of attendance and examination improvement.

Measurement assesses the effectiveness of execution and informs changes to strategy and tactics. Its usefulness depends on alignment with the vision and mission.

### The growth share matrix

The Boston Consulting Group's growth share matrix, also called the BCG matrix or Boston box, classifies offerings by relative market share and market growth.

| Category | Relative market share | Market growth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Star | High | High |
| Question mark | Low | High |
| Dog | Low | Low |
| Cash cow | High | Low |

Stars contribute growing revenue. Question marks require assessment and may need repositioning. Cash cows can generate funds for investment elsewhere with comparatively limited investment needs. The matrix helps reveal portfolio strengths and weaknesses.

Dogs combine limited market share with limited market growth. The categories describe portfolio positions and inform investment discussions.

## From analysis to action

SWOT consolidates evidence into strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats. Strengths and weaknesses arise internally, while opportunities and threats arise externally. A useful SWOT is accurate, concise, and grounded in analysis. Unsupported impressions provide a weak basis for strategic decisions.

Responses to external conditions depend on internal capabilities and constraints. Options may include improving existing offerings, entering new markets, increasing market share, or diversifying. Execution then addresses the gap between the current and target states.

For example, rising demand for digital services may create an opportunity, while weak finances or missing skills may restrict the response. Identifying an opportunity and deciding how to pursue it are separate steps.

### Business model canvas

The business model canvas describes how an organisation creates and delivers value through nine connected components: customer segments, value propositions, channels, customer relationships, revenue streams, key resources, key activities, key partners, and cost structure.

A value proposition identifies the customer problem addressed and the benefits offered, including price, quality, availability, choice, and the organisation's reputation.

The canvas connects customer needs and experiences with the activities, resources, income, and costs required to serve them. Comparing current and proposed models helps stakeholders identify changes and assess their strategic fit.

Channels include communication, service delivery, and payment. Partners may supply essential activities or resources, while the cost structure shows whether the proposed arrangement is sustainable.

### Business capability models

A business capability model describes what an organisation can do without specifying its detailed implementation. Capabilities such as payroll management or tutor allocation provide a shared language for discussing change.

The SUAVE criteria describe capabilities as stable, unique, abstract, valuable, and significant to executives. Related capabilities can be organised into groups and further subgroups. Models commonly distinguish strategic capabilities that set direction, core capabilities involving customers, and support capabilities enabling internal operations.

These models help identify gaps, define the scope of change, assess investment, and explore how existing abilities could support new offerings or markets.

### Value streams and operating models

A value stream shows the high-level activities that deliver value to an internal or external stakeholder. A virtual tuition service, for example, may require identifying a need, designing a solution, creating materials, deploying the service, and marketing it. Value streams can connect to more detailed process models.

A target operating model describes how the organisation needs to operate to execute its strategy. Its scope needs agreement because definitions vary. The POPIT framework covers people, organisation, processes, information, and technology. It includes skills, roles, structure, culture, workflows, data, and supporting systems. Comparing this target with current operations informs the change roadmap.

Unlike a capability model's description of organisational abilities, the operating model specifies the arrangements needed to put those abilities into practice in the intended future state.

Technology choices also involve scalability and interoperability. Data arrangements require attention to capture, storage, analysis, and security. Considering the whole operating model reduces the risk of treating organisational change as a technology decision alone.

## Enterprise architecture

Enterprise architecture describes the organisation's fundamental components, their relationships, and the principles guiding their development. Every organisation has an architecture, whether formally documented or otherwise. It connects strategy with processes, systems, and technology so that individual projects contribute to longer-term capabilities.

| Domain | Scope |
| --- | --- |
| Business | Strategy, structure, culture, operating models, value propositions, processes, capabilities, and information. |
| Applications | Software and other applications, including paper-based arrangements, that support business activities. |
| Data | Information held and used, its relationship to business activities, and where it is stored. |
| Infrastructure | Hardware, operating systems, networks, telecommunications, and cloud services. |
| Compliance | External legal and regulatory obligations, internal policies, and the arrangements that meet them. |
| Security | Physical, technical, and organisational measures protecting assets from harm or loss. |

Compliance and security extend across the other domains. A change to an application can therefore affect processes, data, infrastructure, and protective controls. Architectural awareness helps analysts identify these relationships and avoid solutions that meet immediate needs while weakening future capability.

A small trader and a global retailer both have these domains, although their complexity differs substantially.

Strategy execution changes the current architecture towards the target architecture. Performance measures show whether those changes advance strategic objectives.

## Keeping strategy aligned

Strategic alignment depends on consistency between the organisation's purpose, activities, behaviour, stakeholder expectations, and chosen route to success.

Its actual purpose needs to agree with its stated vision and mission. A cafe promoting nutritious food may create confusion if decisions consistently prioritise profit at the expense of nutrition. Diversification itself need not be harmful, but an unacknowledged change in purpose can leave departments pursuing conflicting goals.

Activities can evolve while remaining consistent with purpose. The same cafe could add home delivery, frozen meals, or cooking workshops. Shared direction also helps avoid fragmented investment, such as separate ordering applications that duplicate processes and data across related businesses.

Consistent behaviour gives practical effect to organisational values. Claims of excellent customer service can damage trust when decisions repeatedly reduce service quality. Analysts need to understand actual decision drivers as well as formal commitments.

Stakeholder expectations require continuing review. Customers, owners, employees, managers, partners, suppliers, competitors, and regulators may influence strategic choices and execution. Feedback helps identify changing needs and likely responses. Failure to recognise these expectations can undermine demand, reputation, or permission to operate.

Leaders determine the route to success by connecting these considerations with environmental conditions and organisational capabilities. Business analysts support that alignment by assessing whether proposed changes, resources, architecture, and measures contribute to the organisation's direction.
