# Chapter 1: What is Business Analysis?

Business analysis helps organisations identify, assess, and implement changes that meet business needs. It examines the whole business system, including people, processes, organisational arrangements, information, and technology. Its contribution extends from investigating problems and comparing options to supporting implementation and assessing benefits. Technology can enable improvement, but successful change also depends on how an organisation works.

## Origins and purpose

The discipline developed as organisations sought better results from investment in technology. IT expanded from supporting operations and management decisions to enabling new products, services, and business models. Yet unclear requirements, poor communication, and insufficient organisational change often prevented systems from producing their expected benefits.

Technology can support competitive advantage through closer connections with customers and suppliers, wider market access, and more flexible operations. Realising these opportunities requires coordination between technology choices and the organisation's broader capabilities.

Outsourcing increased the need for effective business representation. Specialist suppliers offered expertise and potential cost savings, but separation between developers and business staff could complicate requirements definition. Business analysis helped connect technical work with organisational needs.

Internal analysts also developed a consulting role. They brought knowledge of the organisation, retained expertise internally, and remained involved with the consequences of recommendations. External consultants could offer broader experience and an independent perspective, although cost, accountability, and knowledge transfer remained considerations.

These developments reinforced three conditions for effective technology investment: business needs guide development, requirements are sufficiently understood, and implementation includes the necessary business changes. Choosing a technical solution before investigating the problem can undermine all three.

## Scope and guiding principles

Business analysis connects strategic objectives with practical changes. Senior management generally leads strategy development, while analysts help establish how the strategy can be implemented. They need to understand enterprise architecture, including the business, application, and data arrangements that shape proposed changes.

Collaboration with architecture specialists helps analysts understand dependencies and assess whether proposed changes fit the organisation's overall design.

IT systems analysis concentrates on software requirements, including data, processing, interfaces, and non-functional requirements for system quality and operation. Business analysis has a broader focus on outcomes and possible responses. Responsibilities overlap, and organisations allocate them differently.

Assignments can address a local problem, compare substantial business changes, assess a software product, or improve an entire stream of work. Initial investigation establishes whether the stated problem reflects its underlying causes and whether proposed solutions address them.

Six principles guide this work:

- Investigating underlying causes and the wider business context.
- Pursuing business improvement, innovation, and better customer experiences.
- Comparing feasible options and questioning assumptions behind preferred solutions.
- Assessing requirements against business objectives, budgets, and time constraints.
- Supporting the complete change lifecycle, including testing, deployment, and benefits review.
- Negotiating conflicting stakeholder perspectives and requirements.

The business change lifecycle connects alignment, definition, design, implementation, and benefits realisation around a business case. Early analysis aligns proposals with strategy, enterprise architecture, and the external environment. Later analysis supports solution development, business acceptance testing, adoption, and review of actual benefits.

Analysis therefore continues beyond requirements documentation. A solution can function technically yet fail to improve performance if adoption, working practices, or other necessary changes receive insufficient attention.

## Understanding the whole business system

The POPIT model, developed by Assist Knowledge Development, identifies five connected aspects of a business system:

- People: skills, motivation, support, and understanding of business objectives.
- Organisation: roles, responsibilities, management style, culture, and collaboration.
- Processes: efficient working practices, clear handovers, and performance that meets customer needs.
- Information: access to accurate, timely information for operational work and decisions.
- Technology: usable, accessible systems that support staff and customers.

Relationships between these aspects also influence performance. Effective software and processes may produce limited benefits if staff lack training or organisational arrangements obstruct their use.

A whole-system view also broadens the available responses. An urgent service problem may initially require a manual procedure or staff training. Automation may follow when resources and time permit. The appropriate change depends on the business need and constraints.

## Services and value

A service perspective clarifies what business analysts offer. Each service has an intended benefit, a set of activities, and techniques and standards for performing them. The Business Analysis Service Framework, developed by Debra Paul (2018), identifies six core services.

| Service | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Situation investigation and problem analysis | Establishes the business situation and underlying problems or opportunities. |
| Feasibility assessment and business case development | Compares options and establishes the case for investment. |
| Business process improvement | Develops more effective ways of working. |
| Requirements definition | Establishes what a change or solution needs to achieve. |
| Business acceptance testing | Assesses whether a solution meets business needs. |
| Business change deployment | Supports the introduction and use of changes. |

Some analysts also support strategy analysis and execution or specify detailed IT requirements. An organisation may employ specialists in particular services or expect analysts to provide several services across an assignment.

Value depends on how recipients use and experience a service. Completing analysis or delivering a product does not guarantee beneficial outcomes. Analysts and their customers share responsibility for identifying opportunities, developing suitable solutions, and embedding those solutions in everyday work. This collaboration is known as value co-creation.

Clear service definitions help address uncertainty about the analyst's role. Without them, organisations may underuse analytical capabilities or confine analysts to documenting decisions already made.

Role ambiguity can also weaken collaboration because colleagues do not know what to expect. A service portfolio makes the analyst's contribution more explicit while allowing responsibilities to vary with organisational needs.

## Agile working and role variants

Agile development emphasises collaboration, iterative development, and incremental delivery. Requirements can begin at an outline level and become more detailed through investigation, prototypes, and feedback. Analysts adapt their techniques and documentation to the development context.

Agile approaches emerged partly in response to the limitations of completing development stages in a fixed sequence. Their emphasis on adaptation requires sufficient early understanding without assuming that every detail can be settled in advance.

The 2001 Agile Manifesto gives priority to people and interaction, working software, customer collaboration, and responsiveness to change. It still recognises the value of processes, tools, documentation, contracts, and plans.

Direct communication between developers and business staff does not remove the need for analytical work. Analysts can uncover unstated knowledge, challenge assumptions, examine scenarios, and clarify priorities. Early investigation establishes business needs, while continuing analysis connects emerging requirements with strategic objectives.

An analyst may support a product owner, hold that role, or contribute analytical skills within a development team. The allocation of responsibilities depends on organisational circumstances. Analysis also supports linear development approaches, and evolving requirements occur in process and service changes as well as software projects.

Role titles reflect differences in focus, although their boundaries vary.

| Role | Main focus |
| --- | --- |
| Enterprise or business-focused analyst | Business situations, organisational requirements, and redesign of business systems. |
| Technical business analyst or business systems analyst | Software requirements and models of system features, data, and events. |
| Digital business analyst | Digital opportunities, data, innovation, and customer experience. |
| Project business analyst | User stories, business rules, and non-functional requirements for software development. |
| Proxy product owner | Product requirements and feature priorities, with delegated authority to make product decisions. |

Digital roles particularly require analysts to combine business knowledge with technology and data skills. This can create development needs for practitioners whose experience has primarily been in business operations.

## Maturity and authority

The Business Analysis Maturity Model, developed by Assist Knowledge Development, considers both the scope of analytical work and the analyst's authority. It identifies three levels:

1. System improvement: defining requirements for a particular IT change.
2. Process improvement: working across functions to improve business processes.
3. Business improvement: supporting broader organisational change and advising senior management on customer value.

These levels can describe individual analysts, organisational capability, or the profession. A wider remit requires suitable authority and skills.

Process maturity is a separate dimension. Capability Maturity Model Integration, a framework for process improvement, can apply at any of these levels. An adapted progression for business analysis moves from isolated work towards shared processes, standards tailored to context, a monitored service, and continuous improvement. A broad remit alone does not demonstrate mature practice.

## Professional practice and developing skills

Professionalism rests on representative bodies, qualifications, practice standards, and continuing development. BCS (the Chartered Institute for IT) and the International Institute of Business Analysis support these foundations through professional guidance, conduct expectations, and certification. Shared modelling conventions and document standards help organisations maintain consistent practice.

Qualifications recognise knowledge and expertise, while continuing development helps practitioners maintain proficiency as methods and organisational demands evolve. Credentials sit alongside practical competence and professional conduct.

Workforce research by the Institute for the Future for the University of Phoenix Research Institute (2011) and the World Economic Forum (2016) anticipated demand for capabilities relevant to business analysis. These included complex problem solving, critical thinking, creativity, collaboration, and adaptability, alongside cross-cultural work, data interpretation, and virtual collaboration. These capabilities complement business knowledge and technical competence.

Five broad skill areas support effective analysis:

- Stakeholder engagement: collaboration, emotional intelligence, cultural awareness, and understanding others' perspectives.
- Analytical thinking: interpreting data, modelling, evaluating evidence, and prioritising information.
- Innovative thinking: investigating causes, solving problems, generating ideas, and adapting approaches.
- Technical literacy: understanding developments such as artificial intelligence, virtual reality, and robotics.
- Value co-creation: applying service thinking, design thinking, lean thinking, and systems thinking to beneficial change.

Analysts need opportunities to develop these capabilities. Organisations also need to define their responsibilities and give them sufficient authority to investigate problems, question assumptions, and support change. The contribution of business analysis depends on both professional capability and the organisation's willingness to use it.
