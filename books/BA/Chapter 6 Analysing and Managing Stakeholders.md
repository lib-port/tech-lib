# Chapter 6: Analysing and Managing Stakeholders

Stakeholder management helps business change projects understand competing interests, secure appropriate involvement, and manage expectations. Overlooking an influential person or misunderstanding a group's concerns can obstruct change. Effective engagement begins during project initiation and continues through implementation and review of the outcome.

The core activities are identifying stakeholders, analysing their positions, planning engagement, and revising that approach as circumstances change. A project manager or senior business analyst may lead this work, while all team members contribute to understanding stakeholders and maintaining relationships.

## Identifying stakeholders

A stakeholder is anyone interested in a situation, affected by it, or able to influence it. Common categories include customers, partners, suppliers, regulators, employees, managers, owners, and competitors. Other groups, such as insurers, unions, or staff associations, may also be relevant.

Customers receive products or services, partners act on the organisation's behalf, and suppliers provide resources. Regulators oversee compliance, while owners may be proprietors, partners, or shareholders. Competitors may respond to changes with alternative offers or attempts to obstruct an initiative. These relationships influence both the effects of change and the engagement required.

These categories provide a starting point. Customers may differ by market or purchasing behaviour, while managers at different levels may have conflicting priorities. Workshops involving people familiar with the organisation and proposed change can reveal overlooked stakeholders.

Individual influence also differs from collective influence. Customers, employees, and other groups can gain substantial power through coordinated action, unions, publicity, or social media. Limited individual authority does not imply limited collective power.

## Assessing power and interest

A power/interest grid assesses stakeholders by their interest in the project and their ability to support or obstruct it. Positions should reflect actual circumstances, including informal influence. The grid guides engagement but does not replace individual judgement.

| Power or influence | Interest | Engagement approach |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Low | Low | Occasional updates usually suffice, with attention to changing circumstances. |
| Low | Moderate or high | Frequent information explains the reasons for change, its benefits, and its consequences. |
| Moderate | Any level | Relevant updates and involvement help maintain support. |
| High | Low | Monitoring emerging concerns and timely discussion help maintain a constructive relationship. |
| High | Moderate | Regular contact addresses concerns and maintains satisfaction. Greater involvement may help the project. |
| High | High | Frequent discussion keeps key decision-makers involved in decisions and informed about progress. |

Stakeholders strongly affected by change may become frustrated or resist it when they have little influence over decisions. Clear communication needs to explain the proposal honestly. Powerful supporters need continuing engagement, while understanding opponents' concerns may reveal acceptable benefits or changes. Where opposition persists, support from other influential stakeholders may help overcome it.

## Maintaining engagement

Stakeholder positions change with promotions, organisational priorities, external events, and project developments. Analysis therefore requires regular review, including after implementation to assess reactions and outcomes.

A stakeholder plan records names, roles, power, interest, concerns, and current attitudes. It also identifies desired support, intended responsibilities, actions sought, key messages, and planned communications. Attitudes can be classified as champion, supporter, neutral, critic, opponent, or blocker. Champions actively promote success, while supporters approve without necessarily acting. Critics disapprove, opponents actively resist, and blockers obstruct progress, sometimes for unrelated reasons. A neutral stakeholder has expressed no position.

Communication should reflect each stakeholder's concerns. Meetings, targeted updates, and defined project roles can encourage useful involvement. The team needs to evaluate whether these actions achieve the intended response and adjust its approach when necessary. Final recommendations should not surprise the people expected to approve them.

## Clarifying responsibilities

A RACI matrix connects tasks or deliverables with the people involved. Tasks appear in rows and stakeholders or roles in columns. Each designation identifies a different responsibility:

- Responsible: performs the task or produces the deliverable.
- Accountable: answers for the quality of the work.
- Consulted: provides relevant information or advice.
- Informed: receives updates without necessarily contributing directly.

RASCI adds a supportive role, providing assistance or resources to whoever performs the work. Distinguishing these roles clarifies participation and accountability across a project.

For example, a project manager may prepare a business case while the sponsor remains accountable for it. Users and specialists may contribute information.

## Communicating through digital platforms

Social media and internal collaboration platforms can support updates, meetings, shared documents, and discussion. Informality does not remove the need to match content and format to the audience and purpose. Timely, accurate information can build understanding, while inappropriate disclosure can expose sensitive information.

Professional websites and networking profiles may clarify a stakeholder's role and interests. Examining personal photographs or messages can be intrusive. Research needs to remain relevant to the professional relationship.

## Understanding different perspectives

Stakeholders' values and beliefs shape how they define problems and judge proposed changes. Apparent agreement about an organisation's purpose can conceal incompatible priorities. For example, one manager may prioritise winning new customers, while another emphasises service and repeat business.

Systems thinking examines an organisation as a connected whole. Peter Checkland's Soft Systems Methodology (SSM) helps explore complex situations in which stakeholders disagree about problems or possible improvements. It identifies the situation, gathers perspectives, develops conceptual models, compares them with current practice, and defines improvement actions around an agreed model.

### CATWOE

CATWOE explores six connected elements of a stakeholder's perspective. Analysis can usefully begin with world view, despite its position in the acronym.

| Element | Focus |
| --- | --- |
| World view | Beliefs about the system's purpose and what makes it successful. |
| Transformation | The central activity that converts inputs into outputs. |
| Customers | Recipients or beneficiaries of the transformation. |
| Actors | People needed to perform the transformation. |
| Owner | The person or group able to change or close the system. |
| Environment | External constraints and relevant internal requirements, such as governance policies. |

Different world views can imply different customers, activities, and staffing needs. Environmental constraints may include political, economic, social, technological, legal, and environmental factors. Owners and constraints may be shared across perspectives, but this cannot be assumed. Making disagreements explicit supports negotiation. Fundamental differences over purpose or values may require the owner's decision.

### Business activity models

A business activity model (BAM) describes the activities needed to fulfil a stakeholder's perspective. It shows what the business system should do. A business process model examines how activities are performed. A BAM need not describe current practice.

Each distinct perspective initially has its own model, built from five activity types:

- Doing: the central work, derived from CATWOE's transformation.
- Enabling: activities that provide necessary resources.
- Planning: activities that establish requirements and performance targets.
- Monitoring: activities that assess results against those targets.
- Control: corrective action responding to monitoring results.

For a drinks supplier, both a sales manager and a customer services manager may identify selling drinks as the central activity. Their supporting activities can still differ. A focus on new customers may require prospecting and sales targets, while a focus on repeat business may require staff who advise established customers. Their models can expose different resource requirements and performance measures.

Comparing models reveals agreements and conflicts. Negotiation can produce a consensus BAM, although unresolved differences may require the owner to select or combine perspectives. An imposed model may leave some stakeholders unconvinced.

The agreed model supports gap analysis by exposing differences between required activities and current practice. Related activities can also be examined as a thread, such as staffing requirements, recruitment, performance monitoring, and corrective action. This can reveal weaknesses in roles, recruitment methods, or targets. Process modelling can then examine how the required activities should operate.
