# Chapter 5: Investigating the Business Situation

Business investigation identifies problems, examines their underlying causes, and develops options for improvement. It combines qualitative evidence about experiences and perspectives with quantitative evidence about volumes, frequencies, and performance. Diagrams help organise findings and distinguish symptoms from possible causes.

These methods also support process improvement and requirements definition. Understanding existing work helps establish what a future process or system needs to achieve. Because assignments differ in scope, complexity, and organisational context, effective investigation depends on selecting suitable techniques from a broad toolkit. No single method provides a complete account of the situation.

## Establishing the context

Background research gives analysts an initial understanding of the organisation before detailed consultation begins. Four sources are particularly useful:

- The organisation's website reveals its stated mission, values, products, services, and intended image. Navigation and transaction facilities suggest how technology supports those aims. Customer reviews can raise questions, although reviews selected by the organisation may favour positive experiences.
- Company reports reveal financial performance, strategic intentions, target markets, and senior management priorities. Financial statements can expose concerns about debt, liquidity, or declining performance that affect the assignment.
- Procedure manuals and system documentation explain terminology and intended processes. They may have fallen behind actual practice, so their descriptions require checking through investigation.
- Organisation charts identify roles, reporting lines, management responsibilities, and the balance between central and local control. They can also suggest aspects of organisational culture.

Information absent from public sources can also prompt questions. Independent customer reviews may broaden the picture, while the organisation's presentation can suggest its intended market position without establishing the quality of its actual services.

Architectural information adds further context. Business principles establish guiding rules and values. Business capability models describe what the organisation can do, independently of its reporting structure. Roadmaps connect current capabilities with a target operating model, while technology reference models define relevant platforms and standards.

Together, these sources reveal priorities and constraints, expose assumptions, and provide useful starting questions. Background research prepares the investigation but does not replace evidence about how the organisation operates.

## Collaborative investigation

### Workshops

Workshops bring stakeholders together to explore issues, reconcile perspectives, and define requirements. They can also support process modelling, performance analysis, and review of proposed changes. A shared discussion can be faster than separate interviews and can strengthen participants' commitment to agreed outcomes.

Their effectiveness depends on preparation and facilitation. Organising attendance takes time, dominant participants can suppress disagreement, and decisions may be reversed if participants lack authority. The sponsor and facilitator therefore need a realistic objective, suitable participants, an appropriate venue, and a structure that allows everyone to contribute. Larger objectives can be divided into separate sessions.

Where disagreement is likely, a sponsor can establish behavioural expectations and confirm support for the facilitator. Relevant stakeholders need representation, and the setting should minimise interruptions. Participation methods should accommodate people who prefer reflection or small groups to spontaneous discussion.

Different discovery techniques suit different groups:

- Brainstorming gathers spoken ideas and postpones evaluation until suggestions are complete.
- Round robin discussions give each participant an uninterrupted turn.
- Brainwriting allows participants to develop ideas in writing and build on others' contributions.
- Sticky-note exercises gather individual suggestions before grouping related ideas into themes.
- Stepwise refinement repeatedly asks why, exploring the reasoning beneath a statement.
- Break-out groups support detailed discussion before reporting to the full group.

Evaluation follows discovery. Edward de Bono's Six Hats approach separates perspectives: green generates ideas, red explores feelings, white examines facts, yellow considers benefits, black examines risks, and blue manages the discussion. Missing evidence may require further investigation before agreement is possible.

The objective also shapes the analysis. A strategy workshop may identify critical success factors, meaning the conditions essential to success, and key performance indicators that measure progress. Agreement on targets then exposes the information needed to monitor performance. A requirements workshop may use process models and focused discussions to establish functions and data needs.

A separate note-taker lets the facilitator concentrate on participation, non-verbal responses, and progress. Visible records help participants refer back to earlier ideas. The closing discussion confirms conclusions and assigns each action an owner and deadline. Records should be circulated promptly.

Hothouse workshops provide an intensive variation, commonly lasting two or three days. Business representatives and development specialists work through repeated cycles of prototyping and feedback, often in small teams. Particularly useful for innovation, they combine Agile iteration with Lean attention to waste. Their outcomes include a prototype and analysis of the processes, measures, costs, and benefits needed for implementation.

### Focus groups

Focus groups gather experiences and opinions from people with a relevant shared interest. Unlike workshops seeking agreement, they allow views to remain different. They can explore dissatisfaction, reactions to services, or ideas for future development.

Careful participant selection, clear ground rules, and questions grounded in concrete experiences improve discussion. The facilitator protects quieter participants and makes disagreement safe.

Findings are qualitative and do not establish how common an opinion is across the wider population. A group from one location cannot represent every customer. Expressed preferences also require evaluation against business strategy. Focus groups can reveal concerns efficiently, but do not necessarily produce solutions or an agreed course of action.

## Understanding individual work and perspectives

### Interviews

Interviews build relationships and explore responsibilities, concerns, current difficulties, and desired improvements. Individual meetings can reveal issues that staff hesitate to discuss with colleagues. Small group interviews save time, but inhibited participation may require separate follow-up.

Requirements discussions distinguish existing functions that must continue, problems that need correction, and additional capabilities. The last category can be difficult because participants are imagining work beyond their experience. Careful questioning develops vague suggestions into clearer needs without assuming that an initial request fully expresses the underlying problem.

Preparation addresses five questions: why the interview is needed, what information is sought, who can provide it, when it should occur, and where it should be held. An agenda sent in advance supports preparation. An initial meeting at the interviewee's workplace can provide access to documents and insight into working conditions.

The STOP model distinguishes three levels of questioning. Strategic discussions address objectives, scope, and alignment with organisational direction. Tactical discussions concern targets, management controls, and process responsibilities. Operational discussions examine detailed tasks, documents, bottlenecks, and workarounds. Staff estimates of activity require quantitative confirmation.

Senior managers can establish the wider business context and identify staff with detailed knowledge. Meetings of about an hour are often easier to fit into busy schedules and sustain than longer sessions. Follow-up conversations can address unresolved questions without overloading the initial interview.

An interview comprises an introduction, a substantive discussion, and closure. The introduction establishes purpose and context. Discussion can begin with an overview before exploring details, with notes or diagrams maintaining structure. Recording can preserve detail, but may inhibit participants and require substantial review time.

The close confirms key points, actions, deadlines, and arrangements for further contact. Notes should be completed promptly and checked by the interviewee. Agreed records support later requirements work and traceability between identified needs and delivered features.

Interviews require time, and their findings reflect individual perspectives. Conflicting accounts need investigation rather than automatic acceptance of the most confident speaker.

### Observation

Observation reveals actual practices, physical conditions, and tacit knowledge that staff use without thinking to explain it. It can expose difficulties, generate interview questions, and help assess whether proposed changes would work in practice.

Staff need advance reassurance that observation concerns the task rather than personal performance. Relevant workplace and union arrangements require attention. Even then, people may change their behaviour while watched. A short session may also miss infrequent tasks or exceptions.

| Method | Main contribution |
| --- | --- |
| Formal observation | Examines a particular task, including its sequence, timing, variations, and working conditions. |
| Protocol analysis | Combines task performance with explanation, revealing knowledge that is difficult to describe separately. |
| Shadowing | Follows a person through their work, exposing connections between tasks, information, and responsibilities. |
| Ethnographic study | Uses extended time in the workplace to understand culture, informal influence, and complex expert judgements. |

Repeated observation can broaden understanding. Questions should remain neutral and avoid disrupting the work. Ethnographic studies can provide depth, although their duration may exceed available budgets.

Shadowing also provides time to build rapport and discover routine activities that staff overlook in an interview. Physical layouts, interruptions, and the information used during a task can explain difficulties that a written procedure leaves unclear.

### Storytelling

Accounts of failures, frustrations, achievements, and successful teamwork reveal expectations and organisational culture. Informal individual or group conversations can complement structured investigation. The aim is to understand experience and identify recurring patterns, while avoiding blame. Positive stories reveal sources of pride as well as practices that staff value.

## Exploring processes and requirements

### Scenarios

A scenario describes a task or transaction from its triggering event to its outcome. It identifies the actor, sequence of actions, exceptions, and relevant conditions. Preconditions specify what must be true before the task begins. Postconditions describe the required state at completion.

Development begins with the normal successful sequence. Each transition is then examined to establish what must be true for work to continue and what happens if that condition fails. This exposes alternative paths and knowledge that participants might otherwise leave unstated.

For a telephone order, the usual sequence might include identifying the customer, recording items, accepting payment, and confirming delivery. If stock is unavailable, the process may require a substitute, delayed fulfilment, or cancellation. These alternatives become extensions to the main scenario.

Concrete stories can reveal gaps that abstract descriptions miss. A restaurant booking for a particular time, for example, prompts consideration of alternative times or dates when no table is available.

Scenarios support requirements analysis, prototypes, and test scripts. They can be recorded through written descriptions, activity diagrams, decision tables, or storyboards. Complex scenarios take time to develop, and separate descriptions of alternative paths can improve clarity.

Workshop groups can test scenarios by deliberately constructing cases that depart from the normal path. This encourages closer examination of assumptions and can reveal whether proposed procedures fit established working practices.

### Prototypes

Prototypes simulate a proposed process or system so that participants can examine its behaviour and identify requirements, errors, or omissions. They range from paper sketches and linked screen images to working software. Wireframes concentrate on layout and navigation, usually without detailed visual decoration.

The appropriate form depends on scope, fidelity, and functionality. A prototype may cover one screen or an entire process, resemble the eventual solution closely or roughly, and use demonstration or operational data. Scenarios provide a useful basis because they describe the work being represented.

Early paper models can encourage exploration with little development effort. More detailed simulations support examination of specific behaviour. Fidelity therefore reflects the purpose of the exercise, rather than serving as a simple measure of progress towards a finished system.

Prototypes help clarify functions, usability, accessibility, navigation, and performance expectations. Some are discarded after requirements have been checked. Others evolve through development.

Clear objectives prevent endless revisions. Participants also need to understand what remains unfinished. A polished demonstration can create an impression that delivery is imminent, while a prototype using a few records may respond much faster than a shared operational system. These differences need to be reflected in expectations.

Agile development uses repeated, fixed periods of work to validate requirements and build, test, and release functionality. Prioritisation allows less urgent capabilities to move to later iterations while critical functions are delivered earlier.

### User roles and personas

User role analysis groups people according to the services they need, such as customer, employee, or supplier services. One individual may occupy several roles. Roles reveal common requirements and support scenarios, use cases, and user stories.

Broad labels can hide different needs. Personas represent recognisable types within a role, such as banking customers who prefer personal contact or need convenient online access. They help explore motivations, service expectations, and accessibility requirements when designing processes and systems.

An employee may also be a customer, using different services in each capacity. Personas further distinguish people within those roles without requiring a separate analysis of every individual.

## Measuring activity and checking evidence

Quantitative investigation establishes the scale of findings, including transaction volumes, complaint frequencies, and numbers of users. It tests estimates and assumptions gathered through qualitative methods, reducing the risk of designing a solution around inaccurate impressions.

### Surveys

Surveys gather limited information from many people when interviews or workshops would be impractical. Effective design establishes the purpose, explains how responses will be used, gathers relevant classification information, and presents clear questions.

Classification information allows comparisons between relevant groups, such as staff with different lengths of service. Its collection should serve the intended analysis. Clear explanations of the survey's purpose can improve participation and confidence in the process.

If anonymity is promised, combinations of details must not identify respondents indirectly. Job role and demographic information can expose identity even when names are omitted. Doubts about anonymity can discourage participation or distort answers.

Questions need consistent, unambiguous response options. Likert scales can express degrees of agreement, while single-choice options should be mutually exclusive and cover all relevant possibilities. Clear time periods are preferable to vague expressions such as "recently". A question about website use also needs an option for people who have never used it.

Well-structured responses support percentages, comparisons, and analysis of patterns. Software can assist, but cannot compensate for unclear questions. Long surveys and weak motivation reduce completion or encourage careless answers. Small or unrepresentative samples can produce misleading conclusions.

### Special purpose records and activity sampling

Special purpose records count defined events over a period, such as complaints, transferred calls, or requests for information. Analysts or staff may record them, sometimes with durations or categories. The approach is simple, but events can be missed. Participants need to understand its purpose, and recording must remain practical alongside normal work.

Automated records can supply useful measurements where systems capture relevant events. When manual recording is necessary, a simple tally may be more reliable in practice than a detailed form that staff cannot consistently complete.

Activity sampling examines how people divide their time. The analyst defines activity categories, including breaks and other duties, selects observation times, and records what each person is doing. The distribution of observations supports estimates of time allocation. Combined with workload data, it can inform business cases and investigation of inefficient processes.

### Document analysis

Completed forms, reports, and other records show how information supports work. Analysis examines who creates and uses them, where data originates, which checks apply, and when documents are required. Volumes, retention, inconsistent terminology, redundant fields, and missing information can reveal further issues. Document analysis complements interviews and observation, and provides a foundation for data modelling.

## Recording findings and diagnosing causes

Reports of interviews and workshops provide an agreed record. Visual methods then help organise evidence, explore relationships, and identify possible causes. Each offers a different view.

| Technique | Purpose and limits |
| --- | --- |
| Rich picture | Brings people, processes, technology, relationships, and culture into one flexible representation. It has no fixed notation. |
| Mind map | Organises issues around a central subject, with branches for major topics and their details. It helps reveal connections. |
| Business process model | Shows tasks, responsible roles, and workflow. It exposes delays, bottlenecks, and duplication, but gives limited insight into culture and politics. |
| Fishbone diagram | Groups possible causes of a defined problem, supporting diagnosis rather than general recording. |

Rich pictures can capture conflicting priorities, workload pressures, strained relationships, and inadequate resources alongside technical issues. Mind maps organise this material more explicitly. Process models clarify how work flows, but need other perspectives to explain the full situation.

Fishbone diagrams, also called Ishikawa diagrams, place the problem at the head and potential causes along branches. Categories may include people, processes, equipment, location, products, and performance measures. They can be adapted to the organisation and used to structure a workshop. Evidence from interviews, observation, and quantitative investigation fills out the branches.

A juice supplier illustrates how these views connect. Customers may value its products while disliking its website and service. Sales staff may face demanding targets, while customer service staff struggle with complaints. A rich picture captures these tensions, a mind map organises the issues, and a process model locates delays. A fishbone diagram then groups possible contributors to dissatisfaction, such as poor navigation, outdated information, competing departmental priorities, and an overly complicated complaints process. These relationships help focus further investigation.

Causes that recur or appear to have substantial effects deserve closer investigation and priority when planning corrective action. Symptoms should not be confused with established explanations.

Method selection remains tied to purpose. The scale of the business area, participants' locations, available time, and the information required influence the balance of methods. Quantitative techniques are particularly useful when claims about workload or performance need confirmation.

Interviews, workshops, and role analysis apply widely. Observation and quantitative records clarify existing operations, while scenarios and prototypes are especially useful for requirements. Combining complementary methods connects stakeholder experience with measurable evidence and a fuller understanding of the causes of business problems.
