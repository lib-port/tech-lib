# Chapter 2: The Competencies of a Business Analyst
# Business analyst competencies

Business analysts investigate problems, assess options, and support decisions about business and technology change. Competence is the ability to perform the role to an appropriate standard, drawing on knowledge, skills, and judgement.

The T-shaped professional combines depth in business analysis with breadth across other disciplines. Specialist techniques provide depth, while business knowledge and interpersonal skills enable collaboration. Competencies therefore fall into three connected areas: personal qualities, business knowledge, and professional techniques. Their relative importance varies with the organisation, assignment, and services required.

An analyst working in an Agile software team may need a different specialist profile from one leading process improvement. Both need to communicate across operational, financial, governance, and technical functions.

## Personal qualities

Communication combines listening, clear explanation, empathy, and an understanding of different perspectives. Analysts adapt their language to business and technical audiences, avoiding terminology that obscures meaning. Trusting working relationships encourage stakeholders to share information, discuss concerns, and consider alternatives.

Influencing requires an understanding of stakeholders' authority, interests, and sources of resistance. Informal networks can be as relevant as formal reporting structures. Political awareness helps analysts recognise how decisions are made and challenge established views constructively. Evidence and explanations need to suit the audience, while unresolved disagreements may require facilitated discussion or senior support.

Facilitation brings structure to group discussion through preparation, clear objectives, and suitable techniques. The facilitator monitors progress and adapts the approach when necessary. Enough subject knowledge is needed to follow the discussion, but specialist expertise is not always essential. The purpose is to help participants reach conclusions without imposing the facilitator's preferences.

Analytical and critical thinking distinguish evidence from assumptions and investigate underlying causes. Attention to detail helps prevent omissions in requirements, business rules, costs, benefits, and change impacts. Analysis remains proportionate to the problem and constraints. Problem solving also requires curiosity, persistence, and realistic evaluation of alternatives.

Systems thinking, service thinking, and design thinking help analysts examine relationships, intended benefits, and possible improvements. Organisational culture and funding can limit which options are workable.

Resilience supports reasoned challenge under pressure. Adaptability helps analysts work with ambiguity, test ideas, and reconsider established methods. Teamwork allows them to combine contributions from business staff, technical specialists, suppliers, and managers.

Leadership involves establishing a direction, building agreement, and supporting action. It operates through personal responsibility, project collaboration, organisational improvement, and contribution to the wider profession. Professionalism combines ethical conduct, respect for colleagues, relevant competence, and continuing development.

## Business knowledge

Commercial awareness connects recommendations with the organisation's sector, competitive pressures, and available resources. Financial understanding supports decisions in commercial, government, and non-profit organisations. Analysts need a working knowledge of budgets, cash flow, financial statements, costs, and investment appraisal. Business cases compare options, costs, benefits, and risks, often with input from accountants and other specialists.

Investment appraisal methods include break-even analysis and discounted cash flow. Analysts need enough understanding to contribute to financial discussions and recognise unsuitable options.

Domain knowledge covers the sector and its specialised activities. Subject matter expertise adds detailed understanding of a particular product, service, or process. Both help analysts use appropriate terminology, recognise constraints, challenge assumptions, and apply useful experience from comparable organisations.

Digital knowledge enables informed discussion with technical colleagues and recognition of opportunities to improve operations or enhance what the organisation offers customers. The required depth varies by role. Relevant areas include artificial intelligence, automation, data, cloud services, infrastructure, development lifecycles, and Agile approaches. Analysts also need to understand the trade-offs between building software and purchasing an existing product.

Knowledge of organisational structures helps assess changes to teams, responsibilities, and handovers. Supplier management requires awareness of procurement and contractual arrangements, including time-and-materials, fixed-price, and risk-and-reward contracts. Communication and relationship skills support effective supplier engagement.

These contracts respectively link payment to effort and deliverables, set an agreed price for specified work, or share project risks in return for potential rewards. Procurement specialists generally lead supplier selection and contracting.

Enterprise architecture coordinates business, data, applications, and infrastructure. Analysts use architectural information to check that proposed changes fit the organisation's wider design. Capability maps and value stream models can reveal improvement opportunities.

## Professional techniques

Analysts need a range of techniques and the judgement to select those suited to the situation.

| Technique | Contribution |
| --- | --- |
| Stakeholder analysis and management | Identifies relevant people, assesses their influence and perspectives, and guides engagement. |
| Strategy analysis | Connects proposed changes with organisational objectives and the actions needed to achieve them. |
| Investigation | Examines business situations and identifies underlying problems. |
| Requirements engineering | Elicits, analyses, and models requirements that guide business and IT solutions. |
| Business modelling | Represents activities, capabilities, value streams, and processes to clarify how work operates. |
| Data modelling | Clarifies information needs, relationships within data, and associated business rules. |
| Idea generation and visualisation | Encourages alternatives and shared understanding through discussion, brainstorming, and visual models. |
| Gap analysis | Compares current and desired conditions, or available products and capabilities against requirements. |
| Benefits management | Plans, monitors, and evaluates the benefits expected from investment. |
| Project management | Supports scoping, planning, resources, dependencies, quality, risks, and progress reporting. |
| Portfolio management | Evaluates and prioritises related projects, considering their dependencies and collective contribution. |

Analysts may manage smaller projects themselves. On larger projects, they still need to understand project controls and plan their analytical work. Portfolio skills become relevant when responsibility extends across several initiatives.

## Developing competence

Development begins with a comparison between required and existing capabilities. Role descriptions, career frameworks, and discussions with managers help establish the skills and proficiency levels needed. The resulting gaps guide a development plan.

Four approaches contribute:

- Training provides structured learning, supported practice, and feedback.
- Personal study broadens knowledge through professional guidance, books, articles, and other resources.
- Work experience develops practical judgement and interpersonal skills, strengthened by coaching or mentoring.
- Industry engagement provides learning through networks, events, conferences, presentations, and professional contributions.

Reflection makes these experiences more useful. Analysts connect new knowledge with previous work, consider where techniques apply, and examine what different approaches might achieve. Behavioural capabilities often require sustained practice because they involve attitudes and judgement as well as knowledge.

Where formal development programs are unavailable, experienced colleagues or practitioners from other organisations can provide guidance.

## Skills frameworks and suitable assignments

The Skills Framework for the Information Age (SFIA), maintained by the SFIA Foundation, defines skills at increasing levels of responsibility. Its seven levels progress from following and assisting to applying, enabling, ensuring and advising, initiating and influencing, and setting strategy. Individual skills occupy the levels relevant to their scope.

A role profile can combine different proficiency levels in business analysis, business modelling, requirements management, and process improvement. An analyst may therefore need greater proficiency in one area than another. SFIAplus adds supporting information about related skills, qualifications, and publications.

The SFIA business analysis profile spanning levels 3 to 6 distinguishes increasing responsibility:

| Level | Main responsibilities |
| --- | --- |
| 3 | Investigates operational needs, contributes to improvement recommendations, and assists with acceptance tests. |
| 4 | Examines stakeholder objectives, identifies options and benefits, supports acceptance testing, and helps select methods. |
| 5 | Takes responsibility for investigations, adapts methods, defines requirements, and develops business cases covering benefits and risks. |
| 6 | Leads analysis across a substantial organisational area, guides strategic decisions, oversees solutions, and supports senior management through change. |

Assignment complexity depends on how clearly the required work and the method are understood. Skill levels can be matched accordingly.

| Required work | Method | Indicative SFIA levels |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Clear | Clear | 3-4: Suitable for a less experienced analyst. |
| Unclear | Clear | 4-5: Experience in clarifying needs. |
| Clear | Unclear | 4-5: Experience in selecting an approach. |
| Unclear | Unclear | 6: Consultancy skills and senior stakeholder engagement. |

An agreed system scope with an established analysis process presents a different challenge from a broad request to improve sales. The latter can require the analyst to clarify objectives, design the investigation, and help senior stakeholders reach agreement.

Matching capability to the assignment avoids placing analysts in situations beyond their competence or repeatedly giving experienced practitioners work that underuses their skills.

## Professional qualifications

BCS (the Chartered Institute for IT) and the International Institute of Business Analysis (IIBA) offer qualifications recognising knowledge, practical application, and experience. BCS pathways include foundational study, practitioner and specialist assessments, diplomas, and recognition of advanced experience. IIBA's Certified Business Analysis Professional (CBAP) and Certification of Capability in Business Analysis (CCBA) combine examinations with experience requirements, with CCBA intended for less experienced candidates.

Qualifications complement practical development. Effective performance depends on applying suitable techniques, understanding the business, and working constructively with people affected by change. Interpersonal skills are especially significant when uncertainty and concern create resistance.
