# Chapter 4: The Business Analysis Service Framework

Business analysis helps organisations understand problems, evaluate options, and implement changes that deliver business benefits. The Business Analysis Service Framework (BASF) defines six services, connecting their purposes with relevant activities and techniques. It clarifies the analyst's role and supports professional development and career progression.

The services require adaptation to each organisation and project, including applicable standards, available resources, and stakeholder perspectives. Their scope extends across organisational structures, people, processes, information, and technology. The framework provides a common structure without prescribing an identical approach for every assignment.

## The six services

### Situation investigation and problem analysis

Analysts investigate business needs and distinguish root causes from symptoms. Initial problem statements may be incomplete or inaccurate, so investigation requires evidence, contextual knowledge, and a willingness to challenge assumptions and consider conflicting perspectives.

Gap analysis compares current and desired conditions. Business activity models describe what activities should exist, while process models show how work is performed. The comparison can reveal missing activities, inadequate processes, or weak information-system support, indicating the scale of change required. Interviews, observation, surveys, workshops, and cause-analysis techniques help establish the problem, scope, and expected benefits. Investigation may also justify rejecting a proposed project.

### Feasibility assessment and business case development

Analysts develop options and assess their business, technical, and financial feasibility. Assessment considers costs, benefits, organisational impacts, risks, and alignment with strategy and values. Risks and impacts may create additional costs.

Unviable options are eliminated, and the remaining alternatives inform investment decisions. A business case explains the rationale for change and the expected benefits. It may need revision as detailed options become clearer. Techniques include cost-benefit analysis, investment appraisal, impact analysis, and risk analysis.

### Business process improvement

A business process begins with an event and ends when its goal is achieved. Process analysis follows work across departments and roles, focusing on customer needs and results.

Analysts model existing and proposed processes, identify gaps, define implementation actions, and examine performance measures. Process modelling, business rules analysis, and value stream analysis help improve efficiency and effectiveness. Supporting IT systems need to align with the revised processes.

### Requirements definition

Requirements establish what a business or IT solution must provide. Meeting them may involve training or clearer roles, substantial process changes, or new technology. Requirements engineering provides a structure for elicitation, analysis, and management without prescribing specific modelling or documentation techniques.

Analysts elicit, record, analyse, prioritise, and manage requirements with business staff, IT specialists, and external stakeholders. Models, prototypes, and user profiles help clarify needs. Quality checks and stakeholder review support shared understanding. Traceability links requirements to the original business need and eventual solution, while keeping them consistent with project scope and strategic goals.

### Business acceptance testing

Acceptance testing checks whether processes, software, and other changes meet defined requirements and business needs. Business staff retain responsibility for this testing.

Analysts help agree the testing scope, develop scenarios and test cases, conduct tests, and record results. They also help managers understand testing needs and allocate staff time despite operational pressures.

### Business change deployment

Deployment supports the transition to new systems and working practices, their adoption, and the realisation of benefits. Analysts share responsibility with other participants.

Their contributions include readiness assessment, transition planning, training, and documentation of new roles and procedures. Support needs to address the emotional effects of change as well as practical requirements. Post-implementation and benefits reviews help assess whether the intended outcomes are being achieved.

## Stakeholder engagement and value

Stakeholder engagement supports all six services. Stakeholders differ in influence, interests, and attitudes towards change. Analysts identify relevant participants, understand competing perspectives, negotiate conflicts, and maintain communication through meetings, workshops, and ongoing collaboration. Power-interest grids and responsibility frameworks can support this work.

Beneficiaries determine the value of improvements and may include internal staff, executives, customers, suppliers, and regulators. Analysts may work directly with staff whose changed practices subsequently improve customers' experiences. Effective analysis therefore considers both immediate participants and ultimate beneficiaries.

## Strategic direction and boundaries

Business analysis connects detailed work with organisational strategy and the target operating model. Capability models, value streams, critical success factors, and key performance indicators help establish that context. Analysts need sufficient depth to investigate causes and define requirements accurately while retaining a clear view of intended outcomes. Installing software or following a method does not itself establish business success.

Agreed terms of reference define the assignment before work begins. OSCAR identifies five elements:

| Element | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| Objectives | Business outcomes and required project results. |
| Scope | Included activities, deliverables, and explicit exclusions. |
| Constraints | Time, budget, policies, and mandatory standards. |
| Authority | The person who approves outputs and confirms completion. |
| Resources | Available people, funding, facilities, and tools. |

Analysts adapt their approach when circumstances or results require alternatives.

## Creative problem solving

Isaksen and Treffinger's creative problem-solving model places investigation before solution selection. Its adapted sequence comprises six stages:

1. Mess finding explores the situation's complexity and competing concerns.
2. Data finding distinguishes opinions from verifiable facts and gathers supporting evidence.
3. Problem finding identifies underlying causes and revisits the initial problem statement.
4. Idea finding generates alternatives through brainstorming, reversed assumptions, and analogies, without rejecting unusual ideas prematurely.
5. Solution finding evaluates alternatives against criteria informed by the earlier investigation.
6. Acceptance finding secures business support for the selected solution.

Rich pictures, mind maps, and fishbone diagrams help organise complex situations. Surveys and activity sampling can then test claims with quantitative evidence.

The sequence helps counter pressure for premature answers. Understanding the problem, evaluating alternatives, and securing acceptance provide a stronger basis for effective business change.
