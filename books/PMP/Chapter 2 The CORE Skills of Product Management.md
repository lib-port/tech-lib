# Chapter 2: The CORE Skills of Product Management

Product managers connect people with different expertise so teams can deliver products that serve users and meet business goals. This work requires distinct skills, although responsibilities vary across organisations.

Martin Eriksson's Venn diagram places product management at the intersection of business, technology, and user experience. Eriksson developed it while building teams at a start-up, as a description of his experience rather than a universal definition. It identifies complementary capabilities within a team, without requiring one product manager to master every specialist role. Coordination may also involve lawyers, salespeople, suppliers, and customers.

The CORE model identifies four skills that support this connective work: communication, organisation, research, and execution.

## Communication: clarity over comfort

Communication is central to product management because it creates shared understanding among people with different perspectives. The guiding principle is clarity over comfort. Uncomfortable exchanges can reveal unclear expectations, while silence can be mistaken for agreement. If an executive expects a feature that has been removed from a launch, leaving that assumption unchallenged can create a serious misunderstanding.

Effective communication depends on establishing understanding, rather than impressive language. Introversion or working in an additional language does not prevent effective communication. Useful practices include asking necessary questions, explaining priorities, coordinating with other teams, responding promptly and thoughtfully to stakeholders, and comparing options and their trade-offs.

## Organisation: team independence

Organisation turns individual interactions into systems that support the whole team. A product manager who continually gives instructions and resolves problems can become a bottleneck, even when personally knowledgeable and persuasive.

Effective organisation brings people, processes, and tools together so the team can prioritise and deliver without constant supervision. Team members understand their work and its purpose, while colleagues elsewhere can access current, understandable information about the team's activities.

A useful measure is whether work could continue during the product manager's extended absence. Recurring problems call for improvements to the underlying system. Where a team cannot change a process directly, it can reconsider how it works within that process.

## Research: understanding users' circumstances

Research connects product decisions to users' needs and the wider world. It includes interviews, informal conversations, online searches, and examination of competing or related products. Teams also learn by regularly using their own products. Without ongoing learning, a team can meet deadlines while losing touch with its users and market.

Users have priorities beyond the company's deadlines, finances, and planned features. Research therefore examines how a product fits into their broader circumstances. Competing products can be assessed by their effects on users, rather than by which features the team could copy.

Weekly learning directly from users or customers, consistent with Teresa Torres's approach to continuous discovery, helps teams maintain this understanding. Decisions need to reflect both business goals and actual user needs, including when those needs differ from what the business would prefer.

## Execution: effort directed towards outcomes

Execution connects daily work with intended benefits for users and the business. Product managers clarify those outcomes, consider alternative ways to achieve them, and prioritise time and resources accordingly. Selecting features first and justifying them afterwards reverses that process.

Activity alone does not establish success, but pursuing an ideal solution without practical effort also prevents progress. Strategic goals need to guide routine planning and decisions.

Necessary work may range from modest supporting tasks to high-level negotiations, including duties outside the formal job description. Its value comes from its contribution to the team's goals. When demands exceed sustainable capacity, the product manager needs to communicate those limits to their manager.

## Technical knowledge in context

Programming and statistical analysis are often labelled "hard" skills, while communication and time management are labelled "soft" skills. This distinction can obscure the judgement involved in technical work and the potential to learn, practise, and evaluate interpersonal skills. Hiring criteria need to reflect actual responsibilities. Coding challenges can exclude capable candidates when coding is unrelated to the job.

Technical requirements vary with the industry, company, and product. Basic knowledge of relevant systems can ease learning, while tasks such as querying databases or making small code changes require specific technical abilities.

Respect among technical colleagues does not require identical expertise. Micromanaging implementation can undermine working relationships, while dishonest delivery estimates signal a problem of trust that extends beyond technical knowledge. Effective collaboration depends on curiosity, communication, and connections between technical work, user needs, and business goals.

Knowledge and interest are distinct. Product managers need to remain engaged with colleagues' work and willing to learn unfamiliar concepts. The appropriate level of expertise follows from the work required, while the CORE skills enable specialists to work together towards shared outcomes.
