# Introduction to Business Analysis

> [!NOTE]
> Explains how business analysis turns organisational needs into measurable change by aligning stakeholders, defining and prioritising requirements, evaluating solutions, managing risk and traceability, and assessing outcomes.

## Business analysis fundamentals

### Purpose and responsibilities

A business analyst works with stakeholders and delivery specialists to establish why change is needed, what outcomes the organisation seeks and which requirements a solution must satisfy. The analyst clarifies assumptions, resolves conflicting expectations and maintains a clear link between needs, requirements, solution components and tests.

The role varies across organisations and initiatives. A business analyst may contribute to strategy, process improvement, product development, technology implementation, regulatory change or operating-model design. Software knowledge often helps, but coding proficiency is not a universal requirement.

### Career development

Job titles and responsibilities differ between organisations. A common, but not universal, progression includes:

- Junior business analyst: supports elicitation, process documentation and basic data analysis
- Business analyst: leads defined analysis activities and manages stakeholder engagement
- Senior business analyst: handles complex initiatives, selects approaches and mentors colleagues
- Lead or principal business analyst: establishes standards, assures quality and coordinates analysis across initiatives
- Practice or executive leader: oversees capability, portfolios, investment priorities and strategic change

Progress depends on demonstrated capability, sector knowledge, delivery experience and leadership, not job title alone.

### Core capabilities

Effective business analysis combines several capabilities:

- Analytical capability: critical thinking, data interpretation, problem-solving and risk assessment
- Interpersonal capability: facilitation, negotiation, conflict resolution and clear communication
- Delivery capability: structured documentation, prioritisation, verification, validation and testing support
- Technical literacy: knowledge of systems, data, reporting and relevant tools
- Business knowledge: understanding of strategy, operations, customers, regulation and commercial constraints

### Standards, learning and credentials

The International Institute of Business Analysis publishes A Guide to the Business Analysis Body of Knowledge, commonly called the BABOK Guide. It defines knowledge areas, tasks, techniques and perspectives that provide a shared professional framework. The framework informs the Entry Certificate in Business Analysis, Certification of Capability in Business Analysis and Certified Business Analysis Professional credentials.

Formal study, vocational programs, professional development and supervised project experience can all build capability. Employers and certification bodies set different education and experience requirements, so analysts should check the criteria that apply to their intended role or credential.

### Specialisation and related roles

Analysts may specialise by industry, such as financial services, health care or retail, or by solution area, such as customer relationship management, enterprise resource planning, analytics or process improvement. Related roles include systems analyst, process analyst, product manager, project manager and data analyst. These roles overlap, but their accountabilities are not interchangeable.

## A practical business analysis approach

Business analysis is iterative. The sequence below provides a useful framework, but analysts adapt the activities to the initiative, delivery method, risk and governance environment.

### 1. Define the need and scope

The analyst describes the problem or opportunity, gathers evidence about the current state and defines the desired outcomes. The analyst then assesses options, constraints, risks, costs and expected benefits. When governance requires a business case, this work supports its development.

Clear scope identifies included and excluded processes, users, locations, systems and outcomes. It also records assumptions, dependencies and decision authority. These boundaries help the team assess proposed changes without treating the initial scope as fixed when new evidence emerges.

### 2. Identify and engage stakeholders

The analyst identifies people and groups who influence the initiative, use the solution, deliver the change or experience its effects. Stakeholder analysis considers each group's interests, authority, expertise, influence and communication needs.

The engagement approach assigns suitable methods, timing and decision points. Regular workshops, reviews and decision records maintain alignment throughout delivery.

### 3. Elicit requirements and evidence

Analysts combine techniques because no single method reveals every need or constraint:

- Interviews explore individual goals, knowledge and concerns
- Workshops develop shared understanding and resolve differences
- Surveys collect input from larger groups
- Observation reveals actual work practices, exceptions and workarounds
- Document and data analysis test claims against existing rules, processes and performance
- Prototypes and experiments expose usability issues and uncertain assumptions

Elicitation produces raw information, not approved requirements. The analyst analyses, refines and confirms the information before treating it as a reliable basis for delivery.

### 4. Specify requirements

The organisation should select artefacts that suit its governance and delivery approach. A business requirements document may record objectives, scope and high-level requirements. A functional specification may describe system behaviour. User stories, use cases, process models, data models, business rules, wireframes and prototypes can add the detail needed for design, delivery and testing.

Document names do not guarantee quality. Each requirement should be clear, feasible, necessary, testable and traceable to a business or stakeholder need. Acceptance criteria should state observable conditions for acceptance.

### 5. Analyse and prioritise

Analysis examines value, feasibility, dependencies, risks, assumptions and conflicts. Technical, financial, operational, legal and schedule constraints may affect the recommended option.

Teams can use MoSCoW to classify requirements as Must Have, Should Have, Could Have or Won't Have for the agreed timebox or scope. The team should define each category and agree who can assign or change priorities. Other methods may suit initiatives that need numerical scoring, economic sequencing or risk-based prioritisation.

### 6. Verify and validate

Verification checks whether requirements meet quality standards, including clarity, consistency, completeness and testability. Validation checks whether the requirements and proposed solution address the underlying need and support the intended outcomes.

Stakeholder reviews, walkthroughs, prototypes, models and acceptance criteria support both activities. Early and repeated review reduces the cost of correcting misunderstandings.

### 7. Maintain traceability and control change

Traceability links needs to requirements, designs, delivery items, tests and outcomes. A requirements traceability matrix can record these relationships, although integrated tools may provide the same control.

Change control records a proposed change, assesses its effects, assigns a decision-maker and communicates the outcome. The appropriate level of control depends on risk and delivery method. Version history and decision records preserve the rationale for accepted and rejected changes.

### 8. Evaluate outcomes

After implementation, the analyst compares results with agreed measures and identifies gaps, unintended effects and further opportunities. The organisation records acceptance or sign-off when its governance requires it. Benefits often emerge over time, so evaluation may continue after the project or release closes.

### Supporting tools

Tools should support the analysis approach rather than determine it. Common uses include:

| Purpose                      | Examples                                    |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Work tracking                | Jira or Azure DevOps                        |
| Collaborative documentation  | Confluence or SharePoint                    |
| Analysis and traceability    | Excel or a requirements management platform |
| Process and system modelling | Visio, Lucidchart or a modelling platform   |
| Data visualisation           | Power BI or Tableau                         |
| Team communication           | Microsoft Teams or Slack                    |

Tool configuration, terminology and access controls vary between organisations. The team should establish ownership, version control and traceability before relying on any platform as an authoritative source.

## Practical applications of business analysis

Business analysis helps organisations convert challenges into evidence-based decisions and measurable change. Analysts combine stakeholder knowledge, operational evidence and structured requirements across many sectors.

### Cross-industry examples

- Retail banking: Analysts investigate service delays, channel gaps and limited personalisation. They may recommend process changes, customer relationship management capabilities and analytics after comparing viable options.
- Manufacturing: Analysts map supply-chain processes, examine bottlenecks and assess inventory, planning and integration options. Suitable changes may reduce stockouts, excess inventory and fulfilment delays.
- Health care: Analysts elicit clinical, administrative, privacy, security and interoperability requirements for digital health records. Implementation also requires workflow redesign, data migration, training and governance.
- Financial services: Analysts assess data quality, definitions and decision processes before recommending business intelligence capabilities. A central platform can improve consistency only when governance and source data support it.

### Gap analysis

Gap analysis compares the current state with a defined future state and identifies the capabilities, resources or changes needed to close the difference.

1. The analyst defines the current state through process mapping, data analysis and stakeholder research.
2. The analyst defines the desired state through measurable outcomes, benchmarks and agreed constraints.
3. The analyst identifies root causes and ranks gaps by value, urgency, risk and dependency.
4. The organisation assigns actions, resources, owners and milestones.
5. The analyst monitors measures and feedback, then recommends further changes where needed.

### Digital strategy and transformation

Digital strategy aligns technology investment with business objectives. Digitisation converts analogue information into digital form. Digitalisation uses digital technology to improve processes or services. Digital transformation changes how an organisation creates value, operates or serves customers. These concepts describe different degrees and types of change, not a mandatory linear sequence.

Successful transformation depends on clear outcomes, customer and employee needs, data and system integration, security, governance, workforce capability and sustained leadership.

### Technology assessment and artificial intelligence

A technology assessment defines requirements and evaluation criteria before comparing products or approaches. It examines functionality, integration, accessibility, security, privacy, compliance, total cost, supplier viability, implementation capacity and expected benefits. Pilots can test uncertain assumptions before wider adoption.

Artificial intelligence can support analysis, automation and decisions, but it introduces risks related to data quality, bias, privacy, security, transparency and accountability. Governance should define the intended use, human oversight, performance measures, monitoring, escalation and retirement conditions. Sharing information with an artificial intelligence service does not make that information public domain. Organisations must still follow privacy, confidentiality, intellectual property and records-management obligations.
