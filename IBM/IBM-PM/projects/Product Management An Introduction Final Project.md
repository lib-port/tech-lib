# *Product Management: An Introduction* Final Project

## **Scenario**

In the bustling heart of Silicon Valley, a new tech company had just appeared on the scene. StreamLean Gaming, led by visionary CEO Claire Henderson, had made a name for itself by developing engaging educational games for children. These games were designed to make learning math fun and accessible. As the company grew, Claire Henderson saw an opportunity to expand her portfolio of math learning activities. Up to this point, she had relied on engineers and built on the success of the initial product launch. However, profits stagnated as other new ideas from competitors emerged. She decided to act and realized it was time to introduce a more structured way of product development.

To bring this vision to life, she turned to VP of Innovation Mark Robinson and gave him a critical task – assembling a dedicated product management team. Mark knew assembling the right team was critical to StreamLean Gaming's success. He set out to find individuals who had the necessary skills and shared the company's passion for education and innovation. After an extensive search, he introduced three remarkable individuals who would play a critical role in this ambitious project.

### **Meet the Team**

- **Product Manager – Emily Dawson:** Emily was an experienced educational game designer with a background in math and a deep understanding of how to make learning fun for kids. She was tasked with creating the vision for the new games. Emily's innovative ideas and commitment to making math engaging were exactly what StreamLean Gaming needed to stand out from the competition.
- **Project Manager – Michael Chen:** Michael was a virtuoso of project management. His attention to detail, organizational skills, and ability to manage complex projects made him the perfect candidate to lead the implementation phase. Michael was excited about the challenge and ready to turn Emily's vision into reality.
- **Marketing Manager – Sarah Rodriguez:** Sarah had a passion for education and a knack for marketing. Her job was to promote the new math-based learning activities to teachers and parents. She recognized the importance of making the games educational and appealing to the target audience.

Once the team was assembled, Mark formulated his mission to create math games that would revolutionize the way kids learn by engaging them and making them effective. But there was still work to be done. They needed a unique selling point that would set them apart from the crowd and connect the things learners like to do with math.

### **Data and Market Research**

The first item on the agenda was data and market research. Sarah tirelessly gathered information from educators and parents to understand their needs and preferences. Emily dove deep into the world of educational psychology, studying how children learn best. With his meticulous approach, Michael created a detailed project plan and liaised with the development team, who were excited to begin developing the games. They visited schools, conducted focus groups, and even entered math competitions to discover what made the games successful.

### **Design and Development Phase**

Armed with the research, Emily began to create the vision for the game. She envisioned a series of interactive adventures set in a magical world where math problems are the keys to unlocking hidden treasures. She wanted to build on this idea by comparing other popular games where players could compete against each other. To take it a step further, she thought of the next level of competition where classes could compete against each other, both locally and nationally. Emily worked closely with the technical team to ensure the games were fun and aligned with educational standards. Michael held the purse strings and made sure the project stayed on track. He coordinated the efforts of designers, developers, and quality assurance testers and ensured all elements meshed seamlessly.

### **We bring the product to the world**

As the development phase neared its end, Sarah shifted her focus to marketing. She developed a strategy that involved presenting the games at educational conferences and reaching out to educational influencers. Sarah was convinced that StreamLean Gaming's math games would quickly gain traction if they created a buzz among educators and parents.

### **The Big Launch**

Months of hard work finally culminated in the launch of StreamLean Gaming's new math-based learning activities. Emily's vision had become a reality, and the games were exactly what she envisioned. Michael's meticulous planning paid off as the launch went smoothly. Sarah's marketing efforts paid off as educators and parents enthusiastically embraced the new educational tools.

StreamLean Gaming's journey to educational innovation was challenging but also very rewarding. The math games they developed were not only educational but also fun, changing the way kids learned and making math an adventure.

Under Claire's leadership, the company continued to evolve, setting new standards in educational technology and proving that innovation, passion, and a dedicated team can revolutionize education for the better. StreamLean Gaming's mission was to make learning math an exciting adventure, and it was more successful than in their wildest dreams.

### Make five observations about what you think could have gone better:

#### Success was not supported by clear measures

The scenario describes a successful launch but gives no targets or results for learning progress, continued use, or financial performance. The account could have explained how the team defined success and assessed the product's impact.

Each goal needed clearly defined measures agreed before development began. Learning required an assessment of the intended math skill, regular practice required evidence of repeated use, and business growth required financial results. Enthusiasm at launch alone could not establish all three outcomes. Without these measures, the team could struggle to decide which features deserved further investment. Defining success before development and reviewing results after launch would help establish whether the product achieved its educational and commercial goals.

#### Direct testing with children was not clearly described

The team consulted adults and visited schools, but the scenario does not clearly describe children testing early versions of the game. Observing children play could reveal difficulties with instructions, navigation, and challenge levels that adult feedback might miss.

For example, children might understand the math but struggle to use the controls, or guess answers to collect rewards. These examples suggest questions the team could investigate through testing. Quality assurance testers were involved, but the account does not explain whether they assessed children's understanding or learning. Examples of learner feedback leading to design changes would have been helpful. Repeated trials and revisions would demonstrate how the team checked its assumptions before completing the full product for launch.

#### The unique selling point needed clearer evidence

The company wanted a unique selling point, but the scenario does not show how it confirmed one. The adventure concept and proposed competitions offered possible directions, yet there is no evidence that customers preferred them to existing alternatives.

The target market also remained broad, without a specified age group, math topic, or purchasing context. Teachers seeking classroom activities could have different needs from parents seeking home practice. A clearer audience would have helped the team select features and explain the product's value. Competitor research and customer testing could then establish whether the proposed benefits addressed a specific need and provided a convincing reason for customers to choose StreamLean Gaming.

#### The commercial plan was not explained

Stagnant profits prompted the initiative, yet the scenario does not explain how the games would improve financial performance. Michael managed the budget, but pricing, payment arrangements, and ongoing support costs remain unclear. Whether the product addressed the original commercial problem cannot to be confirmed.

The company needed to identify who would pay and what they expected. Selling to parents could involve different requirements from selling to schools. Maintenance, additional content, and customer acquisition would also affect costs. An enthusiastic launch could still produce weak returns if revenue failed to cover these expenses. A clearer business model would connect product decisions with growth goals and provide a firmer basis for deciding how much to invest in future features.

#### Child safety and accessibility were not addressed explicitly

The scenario does not explain how the team addressed children's privacy, safety, or accessibility needs. These areas deserved attention because children were the intended users and Emily proposed competition between players and classes. The scenario leaves the final design of those competitive features unclear.

Accessibility raised further questions about instructions, reading demands, and controls. Requirements and testing should have addressed these considerations. Addressing them early could reveal barriers before launch, support more comfortable participation, and build confidence among educators and parents. ### Make five recommendations to improve product management in this company:

#### Create a short product strategy and prioritise the roadmap

Emily could prepare a short product strategy with Michael and Sarah, and review it with Mark. It should identify the initial learners, the learning problem, and the intended benefit, while connecting the product to the company's growth goals.

Emily should use this strategy to prioritise a simple roadmap. She could compare features by learning value, customer evidence, and development effort. Michael should provide development estimates, and Sarah should explain customer demand. The team should also agree who makes each decision. Emily could own product priorities, Michael could manage delivery, and Sarah could own research and promotion. Mark could help resolve investment decisions. Regular reviews would connect priorities with new findings and help explain tradeoffs.

#### Test a small playable version with children and teachers

Testing a small playable version built around one adventure and one math skill is recommended. Emily could assess whether children understand the instructions, enjoy the activity, and practice the intended skill before expanding the game.

Supervised trials should involve children with different abilities and access needs, with appropriate adult permission. Teachers and parents could explain how the activity fits classroom and home use. The team should record problems, revise the game, and test again. Assessments before and after use could indicate learning, although other teaching might also explain improvement. An education specialist could support a stronger evaluation. Recording expectations, observations, and resulting changes would connect feedback to product decisions and help decide when to begin wider testing.

#### Validate the target market and business model together

It is recommended for Sarah and Emily to select a customer group and test the game's value with those customers. They should compare alternatives using criteria such as curriculum fit, ease of use, and price. Interviews and pilots could explore interest and willingness to pay.

A paid trial could test whether customers would buy access. The team should then check continued use. Michael should estimate development, hosting, and support costs, alongside the expense of attracting customers. The team could compare these costs with proposed prices and identify assumptions that need further testing. A business case would connect customer value with commercial viability, helping the team assess whether the product could support profitable growth.

#### Include safety and accessibility in product requirements

It is recommended to include child safety and accessibility requirements for every release. Emily should work with technical colleagues and privacy specialists to identify necessary player information, decide who can access it, and explain its use. Michael should schedule these release checks.

Competition should begin with supervised classroom trials with teachers controlling participation. The team could test rewards for personal improvement and group progress, and observe learners' responses. Accessibility checks should cover readable instructions, usable controls, and information conveyed through more than color alone. Testing should include children with relevant access needs. The team should resolve serious barriers before release. Expansion between schools should require evidence that teachers and the service can manage the additional activity.

#### Manage the product after launch through regular reviews

Monthly product reviews involving Emily, Michael, and Sarah are recommended. The team should agree on measures for learning progress, repeated use, and commercial performance, each with a definition, baseline, and target. The team should consider results alongside feedback from teachers, parents, and children.

Emily could examine learning difficulties, Sarah could assess which marketing channels attract continuing users, and Michael could track delivery and support costs. The team should investigate problems before choosing solutions. Development time for fixes and improvements should be reserved. Reviews should guide decisions to expand features, change them, or stop further investment. This would connect launch success with ongoing learning and help assess whether the games deliver lasting educational and business value.
